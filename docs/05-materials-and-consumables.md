# Materials & Consumables

## Needles

| Use | Needle |
|---|---|
| **Embroidery** | **75/11** home sewing machine needle |
| Knits / stretch (embroidery) | 75/11 **ball point** |
| **Coated / waterproof woven (embroidery)** | **75/11 sharp — never a ball point** |
| Lightweight sewing | 65/9 – 75/11 |
| Medium sewing | 75/11 – 90/14 |
| Heavyweight (denim, canvas) | 90/14 – 100/16 |

The built-in needle threader works with **75/11 through 100/16** and does not
work with twin needles or with monofilament/specialty threads.

**Change the needle every 6–8 hours of stitching, and immediately after any
thread jam.** A blunt or bent needle is the root cause of most skipped stitches,
shredding thread, and bird-nesting. Needles cost pennies; a ruined garment
doesn't. This is the highest-return habit in machine embroidery.

## Thread

| | |
|---|---|
| Top thread | 40 wt polyester or rayon embroidery thread |
| Bobbin thread | **60 wt** embroidery bobbin thread |

Do **not** put 40 wt top thread in the bobbin for embroidery. Bobbin thread is
finer on purpose — it keeps the design's back flat and stops the underside
building up bulk that distorts the face.

### Does the bobbin need to match the top thread colour?

**No — not for embroidery.** Use one dedicated bobbin thread for the whole
design regardless of how many colours are on top.

The SE700 manual draws the distinction itself:

| | Manual says |
|---|---|
| **Sewing** utility stitches | "*Normally, the thread will be the same as what is used for the upper threading*" — so yes, match |
| **Embroidery** | "*be sure to use the recommended **60 weight embroidery bobbin thread***" — a dedicated thread, not the top colour |

The reason is that with correct tension the bobbin thread never reaches the
face: only the top thread shows on the right side, only bobbin thread on the
wrong side. That is also why embroidery bobbin thread is only sold in white and
black — nobody needs it in 64 colours.

**Which of the two:**

- **White** for light fabric and light designs — the default, and what to use
  for a design on white cloth.
- **Black** for dark fabric or a predominantly dark design, so any bobbin that
  does peek at an edge does not flash white.

**When matching *does* matter** — any time the back is seen:

- Freestanding lace (both faces are the front)
- Reversible items, scarves, napkins, towel ends
- Sheer fabric, where the bobbin shows through from behind

Outside those cases, changing bobbins per colour costs time and gains nothing.
If bobbin thread is showing on the front, that is a **tension fault**, not a
colour problem — see [Stitch-Out Playbook](06-stitch-out-playbook.md#tension-quick-reference).

Polyester is the sane default: colourfast, bleach-tolerant, strong. Rayon has
more sheen but is weaker and less wash-tolerant. For anything going through a
washing machine, use polyester.

Brother's own palette is 64 colours (see
[`../reference/charts/brother-pec-thread-palette.csv`](../reference/charts/brother-pec-thread-palette.csv)),
and PES files store colour as an index into it. Third-party thread from Simthread
or New Brothread ships with Brother-code conversion charts and works fine.

## Bobbins

**SA156**, Class 15, 11.5 mm high. The plastic bobbins in the box are the
reference; buy Class 15 to match.

- **Do not use Class 15J** (the slightly different Japanese profile) — it will
  seat but causes tension trouble.
- Metal bobbins are not a substitute; the machine's tension is set for plastic.
- Prewound bobbins are convenient and consistent; check they're 60 wt and Class 15.

Insert with the thread coming off in the correct direction — the `b` mark on the
supplied bobbins is the reference. Reversed bobbin direction is the #1 cause of
bird-nesting.

## Stabilizer

Stabilizer is not optional. Embroidery puts thousands of needle penetrations
into fabric that was never designed to take them; the stabilizer, not the
fabric, carries that load.

| Type | Behaviour | Use on |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut-away** | Stays permanently, trimmed close | Knits, t-shirts, polos, anything stretchy, dense designs |
| **Tear-away** | Torn off after stitching | Stable wovens — denim, canvas, quilting cotton, towels (with topping) |
| **Wash-away / water-soluble** | Dissolves completely | Freestanding lace, cutwork, anywhere the back must be clean |
| **Water-soluble topping** | Sits **on top** of the fabric | Towels, fleece, corduroy, velvet, piqué — anything with pile or loft |
| **Seal-the-back** (Sulky Stitch 'n Seal and equivalents) | Iron-on, applied **after** stitching | Coated and waterproof fabrics — it reseals the needle holes |

Two rules cover most decisions:

1. **Stretch fabric → cut-away.** Tear-away on a t-shirt gives a design that
   looks fine on day one and puckers permanently after the first wash.
2. **Pile or texture → add a water-soluble topping.** Without it the stitches
   sink into the nap and the design reads as fuzzy and half-formed.

Denser designs need heavier stabilizer. Two layers of medium tear-away often
outperform one heavy layer, because you can tear them off in different
directions without stressing the stitches.

**Adhesive/sticky stabilizer** deserves a mention: hoop the stabilizer alone,
score and peel the release paper, and press the item onto it. This is how you
embroider things that cannot be hooped — collars, pockets, socks, baby clothes.

## Coated and waterproof fabrics are their own case

PU- or PVC-coated wovens — waterproof canvas, 600D and 1000D pack cloth, oilcloth,
laminated cotton, waxed canvas — break three of the rules above at once, and the
usual remedies for each are the exact things the coating cannot survive.

**Every needle hole is permanent, and it is a hole in the waterproofing.** A bare
weave partly closes behind a needle; a coating does not. Three consequences:

- **Use the smallest needle that will drive the stack, and keep it sharp.** 75/11
  sharp, not a ball point — a ball point shoves the coating aside instead of
  cutting it. The "use a ball point on polyester" advice everywhere online is
  aimed at *knit* polyester and is wrong for a tight coated woven. A blunt needle
  makes a bigger hole, so here changing it is a waterproofing decision rather
  than a cosmetic one.
- **Design open, not solid.** Fewer penetrations is less perforation. A dense
  fill on a coating perforates it along a line, which is how coated fabric tears.
- **Reseal afterwards if it has to shed weather.** That is what the seal-the-back
  stabilizers in the table above are for — they go on the back after stitching.
  They are **iron-on**, which collides with the next rule; test on a scrap of the
  actual cloth first, and fall back to a brushed-on liquid seam sealer if the
  coating lifts.

**Do not hoop the fabric, and do not iron it.** A hoop ring compresses a coating
and the mark can be permanent — and the standard fix for hoop burn, pressing it
out with a low iron, destroys the coating instead. So:

- **Float it.** Hoop sticky-back tear-away alone, score and peel, press the piece
  onto it. This is the same technique as for anything too small to hoop, and the
  Operation Manual (p.64) describes it.
- **Baste it down. Never pin it.** A pin hole costs exactly what a stitch hole
  costs, and it is outside the design where nothing will ever cover it.
- **Embroider an oversized blank and cut the piece out afterwards**, so the hoop
  never touches the piece at all.
- Test the adhesive too — pulling sticky stabilizer off a coating can lift or
  gloss it.

**No topping.** There is no nap for stitches to sink into, and a topping is one
more thing to pick out of holes you cannot close.

**"Slow the machine down" is the other advice you will find, and this machine
cannot.** Embroidery speed is fixed at 400 spm with no setting — see
[`01-machine-reference.md`](01-machine-reference.md). Do not go looking for it.

practitioner-and-vendor sourced, the same standing as the parameters in
[`16-narrow-material.md`](16-narrow-material.md).

## Hooping

The fabric should be **taut like a drum but not stretched**. Stretching it and
stitching means the design distorts when it relaxes. Tighten the hoop screw with
the fabric *in* it, don't force fabric into an already-tight hoop.

- Hoop the stabilizer and fabric **together**, as one sandwich — *except* on a
  coated or waterproof cloth, where the hoop must never touch the piece at all.
  See the section above.
- Never iron over stitched embroidery directly — press from the back, on a towel.
- Check nothing (sleeve, other side of the garment) is trapped underneath before
  you start. The machine will happily sew a shirt shut.

## Shopping list for a new SE700

Beyond what's in the box:

- 75/11 embroidery needles — a pack of 10 minimum
- Class 15 (SA156) bobbins — a dozen
- 60 wt bobbin thread, white and black, on cones
- 40 wt polyester embroidery thread — a starter set of 40 colours
- Medium **cut-away** stabilizer (the one you'll use most)
- Medium **tear-away** stabilizer
- Water-soluble **topping** — a roll
- Temporary spray adhesive (505 or similar)
- **Sticky-back tear-away** — the one that makes unhoopable things embroiderable
- **Seal-the-back stabilizer**, if anything you make has to stay waterproof
- Small sharp curved scissors for trimming jumps
- Optional but transformative: a **magnetic hoop** for the 4×4 mount. Faster to
  load, kinder to fabric, no hoop burn.
